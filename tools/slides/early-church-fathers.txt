Apostolic Roots & The Pentarchy
The Early Church Structure
The earliest Church was an organic network of local communities. Authority gradually consolidated around
Apostolic Sees
—Cities where the Apostles or their immediate disciples founded major, influential churches.
By the
4th century
(Council of Nicaea, 325 AD), three Sees held primary jurisdiction across broad regions:
Rome, Alexandria, and Antioch .
These were the indisputable ancient powerhouses.
The Imperial
Addition
: The Pentarchy
The
Pentarchy
(rule of five) was a
later imperial plan
to formalize the five supreme Sees, strategically adding Constantinople and Jerusalem.
The system was based on the belief that these Patriarchs, acting
collectively
, safeguarded the universal faith, with authority residing in the
Ecumenical Councils
, not in the singular power of any one See.

1,600 Years of Unity: Alexandria and the Ethiopian Episcopal Line
The Primacy of Alexandria
The
Patriarchate of Alexandria
(the Coptic Church) was one of the three original and most influential
Apostolic Sees
(along with Rome and Antioch).
It was traditionally founded by
St. Mark the Evangelist
, the author of the Gospel of Mark. Alexandria became the intellectual and
theological capital
of early Christianity, wielding immense authority over all of Africa and establishing the standards for biblical scholarship and doctrine.
Ethiopia's Indivisible Link
The First Bishop:
This link began when
St. Frumentius
(Abune Selama) was consecrated as the first Bishop of Aksum by
St. Athanasius, Pope of Alexandria
, around 330 AD.
The Church in the Aksumite Kingdom (modern Ethiopia/Eritrea) was directly under Alexandria's spiritual jurisdiction for
over 1,600 years
.

The Shared Foundation of Faith
All major ancient Christian Churches—Oriental Orthodox, Eastern Orthodox, and Roman Catholic—share and uphold the definitions set forth by the
first three Ecumenical Councils
.
The Tewahedo Church uses these councils as the undisputed foundation for its doctrine on the
Trinity
and the
Incarnation
.
--- Slide 5 ---
The Great Theological Conflict
The Pivotal Question
After the first three councils affirmed that Christ is
fully God
and
fully Man
(and
one Person
), a crucial question remained:
How exactly are His Divine nature and Human nature united in that one Person?
This debate was known as the
Christological Controversy
. Disagreeing on the precise wording to describe this union led directly to the first major split in the ancient Church.
--- Slide 6 ---
Christology Dilema
The Two Main Camps
By the mid-5th century, two main schools of thought dominated the debate:
The Alexandrian School (Tewahedo/Miaphysite)
Focus:
Emphasized the
complete unity
of the natures after the Incarnation. They feared any language that seemed to separate Christ's divinity and humanity.
Doctrine:
Argued that the natures are made "
one
" and work together as a single Divine-Human entity (
Miaphysitism
or
Tewahedo
).
Key Figure:
St. Cyril of Alexandria
(whose formula was central to the Council of Ephesus).
The Antiochian/Western School (Dyophysite)
Focus:
Emphasized the need to clearly affirm that Christ's
divinity and humanity remained distinct
after the Incarnation. They feared language that suggested the Divine swallowed up or confused the Human nature.
Doctrine:
Maintained that Christ exists
"in two natures"
(
Dyophysitism
).
Key Figure:
Pope Leo I of Rome
(whose
Tome
was central to the Council of Chalcedon).
--- Slide 7 ---
The Council of Chalcedon (451 AD)
The Chalcedonian Definition (451 AD)
The Imperial Mandate
Convened by the Roman Emperor
Marcian
in 451 AD, the Council was called to definitively resolve the Christological conflict between Alexandria and Antioch.
The Council affirmed the doctrine that Christ is "
one and the same Son... acknowledged in two natures
" (
in duabus naturis
).
This core statement is known as
Dyophysitism
(Greek for "
two natures
").
The
Definition
Critical Compromise
The Chalcedonian Definition sought to be a balanced compromise by defining
how
the two natures exist within Christ, using
four negative adverbs
to guard against heresy.
The natures were united:
Without Confusion
(
Asynchytos
): To guard against the Alexandrian extreme (which the Dyophysites called "Eutychianism").
Without Change
(
Atreptos
): To guard against the idea that the Divine Nature was altered.
Without Division
(
Adiairetos
): To guard against the Antiochian extreme (Nestorianism).
Without Separation
(
Achoristos
): To guard against the final separation of the Person.
--- Slide 8 ---
The Tewahedo Objection
The Alexandrian Rejection
The Alexandrian (Coptic) delegation, led by
Pope Dioscorus
, absolutely rejected the Chalcedonian phrase "
in two natures
" (
en duo physeis
).
Theological Fear:
They argued that this wording risked a return to the heresy of
Nestorianism
(which divided Christ into two separate persons/subjects).
Core Doctrine:
Alexandria insisted on the formula of
St. Cyril
: "
One Incarnate Nature of God the Word
," believing that
after
the Incarnation, the two natures became
one unified reality
(Tewahedo/Miaphysite).
The Schism:
The Council illegally
deposed and exiled Pope Dioscorus
, effectively marking the permanent administrative and theological schism.
The Result
The Churches that rejected the Council of Chalcedon became known historically as the
Non-Chalcedonian Churches
(or the
Oriental Orthodox
family).
This marks the moment the ancient Apostolic Sees of
Alexandria
(Coptic),
Antioch
(Syriac), and
Aksum
(Tewahedo) officially separated from the Patriarchates of Rome, Constantinople, and Jerusalem.
--- Slide 9 ---
The Tewahedo Christological Stance
Miaphysitism: The Doctrine of Unity
The theological position of the Tewahedo Church and the entire Oriental Orthodox family is
Miaphysitism
(Greek for "one nature").
This doctrine affirms that, after the Incarnation, Christ possesses
one unified Divine-Human nature
(
physis
).
The two natures are perfectly and perpetually joined, making them a single reality.
The Defining Formula
Miaphysitism is based on the formula of
St. Cyril of Alexandria
:
"One Incarnate Nature of God the Word."
To visualize this union, the Tewahedo Fathers used the analogy of
fire and iron
: The fire and iron become one glowing, inseparable piece, but the iron does not cease to be iron, and the fire does not cease to be fire.
--- Slide 10 ---
The Ge'ez Term: Tewahedo
What is
Tewahedo
?
The term
Tewahedo
is the Ge'ez word (the ancient liturgical language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Churches) that translates directly to
"made one"
or
"unity."
It is the native theological equivalent of the Greek
Miaphysitism
.
The term is so central to the Church's identity that it is part of its official name: The Ethiopian/Eritrean Orthodox
Tewahedo
Church.
The Theological Significance
Tewahedo
expresses the core belief that the
Divine Nature
and the
Human Nature
of Jesus Christ are permanently and perfectly united into
One Person
and
One Nature
.
This unity is understood to be
without separation, without confusion, and without change
—both natures remain distinct in their properties but are inseparable in their existence within Christ.

The Nine Saints & Monasticism
Exiles of Faith and Missionaries
Period:
The
late 5th and early 6th centuries
, right after the theological turmoil of the Council of Chalcedon (451 AD).
Identity:
A revered group of missionaries, traditionally numbering
Nine Saints
, who arrived in the Aksumite Kingdom.
The Context:
Many were believed to be Byzantine monks or clerics who fled the Roman Empire due to their
rejection of the Chalcedonian Dyophysite doctrine
. They sought refuge and a fertile ground for their
Miaphysite
faith.
Securing the Tewahedo Identity
Their arrival was critical in solidly establishing the Alexandrian theological position in the Ethiopian Highlands. They were missionaries of the
Tewahedo faith
.
Monasticism:
They introduced and formalized the structures of
intensive monasticism
, creating spiritual centers that became essential for preserving the faith during later centuries of isolation.
Scripture:
The saints were instrumental in finalizing and standardizing the translation of the Bible and key liturgical texts into the classical Ethiopian language,
Ge'ez
.

Monasteries and the Nine Founding Fathers
Centers of Faith and Learning
Each saint is associated with establishing a key monastery that served as a center for theology, education, and cultural preservation, primarily located in the regions of Tigray (Ethiopia) and Eritrea. These monasteries became the strongholds of the Miaphysite faith:
Debre Damo:
Founded by
Abba Aragawi
, this is one of Ethiopia's oldest monasteries, famously located on a flat-topped mountain accessible only by a rope.
Abba Garima Monastery:
Founded by
Abba Garima
, this monastery is the custodian of the
Garima Gospels
, some of the oldest illuminated Christian manuscripts in the world, dated to the 5th–7th centuries.
Debre Zewayt/Qorqor:
Associated with figures like
Abba Aftse
.
The Tewahedo tradition venerates these specific figures as the fathers of Ethiopian monasticism. They are traditionally named:
Abba Aftse
Abba Alef
Abba Aragawi
(or Za-Mika’el)
Abba Garima
Abba Guba
Abba Liqanos
Abba Pantelewon

Abba Yem'ata
