--- Slide 2 ---
Context and Background
Author:
St. Paul the Apostle.
Writing from Corinth around 57 AD, toward the end of his third missionary journey.
Audience:
The Christians in Rome. Unlike other churches Paul wrote to, he did not found this church. It was a mix of Jewish converts and Gentile converts.
The Conflict:
There was tension between Jewish Christians (who felt the Mosaic Law was still necessary for salvation) and Gentile Christians (who felt the Law was obsolete). Paul writes to bring them into
Tewahedo
(unity/oneness) in Christ.
--- Slide 3 ---
The Spiritual Architect: St. Paul as "Church Planter"
Major Sees Established by St. Paul
Corinth (Greece):
A 18-month mission in a cosmopolitan hub; Paul served as their "Spiritual Father" (Acts 18).
Ephesus (Asia Minor):
Paul’s base for 3 years; it later became the home of
St. John the Evangelist
and the
Virgin Mary
(Acts 19).
Galatia (Turkey):
A regional mission (Iconium, Lystra, Derbe) focusing on the transition from Law to Grace.
Philippi (Greece):
The first Church in Europe; founded through the conversion of Lydia and the jailer (Acts 16).
Thessalonica (Greece):
A community that thrived and survived despite intense persecution (Acts 17).
Crete:
Established by Paul and organized by his disciple
St. Titus
(Titus 1:5).
The "Roman Distinction"
When teaching, contrast Paul’s relationship with these churches vs. Rome:
To Founded Churches:
He writes as a
Father
correcting his children (Authority).
To the Romans:
He writes as a
Brother
and a
Guest
, seeking "mutual encouragement" (Humility).
Romans 1:8 shows the church already existed:
"First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for you all, that your faith is spoken of throughout the whole world."
.
--- Slide 4 ---
The Path to Oneness: Resolving the Roman Conflict
In Oriental Orthodoxy, we view "Justification" not just as a legal status, but as
healing
. We were sick with sin, and through Christ, we are made whole.
The Problem:
Both Jews and Gentiles have fallen short. The Law showed us our "sickness" but could not provide the "medicine."
The Solution:
Christ is the Great Physician. By faith—which in our tradition is not just mental belief, but
active, loving obedience
—we are united to Him.
Romans 1:16-17:
"For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ... for in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith."
.
--- Slide 5 ---
The Gospel of Reconciliation:
Jews, Gentiles, and the New Covenant
1. Leveling the Playing Field
Paul starts by essentially telling both groups they are in the same boat.
For the Gentiles:
He argues that even without the Law, they are guilty of turning away from God (Romans 1).
For the Jews:
He argues that having the Law doesn't save you if you don't keep it perfectly (Romans 2).
The Result:
"For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). By making everyone equally "guilty," he removes the ground for any group to feel superior.
Romans 2:25-29: Paul explains that true circumcision is of the heart, by the Spirit, not just the letter of the Law.
(R)
Romans 3:9 "What then? Are we better than they? Not at all. For we have previously charged both Jews and Greeks that they are all under sin."
--- Slide 6 ---
The Gospel of Reconciliation:
Jews, Gentiles, and the New Covenant
2. The Abraham Argument
To appease the Jewish Christians who valued their heritage, Paul uses
Abraham
as his primary example. He points out that Abraham was "counted as righteous" because of his faith
before
he was circumcised. This proved that:
God’s promises weren't just for those under the Mosaic Law.
Gentiles could be "children of Abraham" through faith alone.
Romans 4:3:
"For what does the Scripture say? 'Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.'"
--- Slide 7 ---
The Gospel of Reconciliation:
Jews, Gentiles, and the New Covenant
3. Practical "Oneness" (The Weak and the Strong)
In the later chapters (especially Romans 14), Paul gets into the "nitty-gritty" of their daily conflict:
The Issues:
Dietary laws and the observation of sacred days.
The Solution:
Paul tells the "strong" (those who felt free from the Law) not to look down on the "weak" (those who felt bound by it), and vice versa. He emphasizes that the Kingdom of God isn't about food or drink, but "righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit."
Romans 14:1-3:
"Accept the one whose faith is weak, without quarreling over disputable matters... The one who eats everything must not treat with contempt the one who does not."
Romans 15:7:
"Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring praise to God."

Structural
Breakdown Of Romans: Medical Framework
In our church, Christ is not just a Judge who clears a legal debt; He is the
Medhanit
(Savior/Healer) who restores a broken nature.
The Diagnosis (Chapters 1–3):
St. Paul shows that the whole world is sick with the "fever" of sin.

--- Slide 11 ---
The Diagnosis:
The Fever of Sin (Chapters 1–3)
Before a doctor can prescribe medicine, they must show the patient they are sick. St. Paul performs a "spiritual check-up" on the entire human race.
The Symptoms:
Paul lists the symptoms of humanity’s sickness—idolatry, pride, and moral decay.
The Lab Results:
He concludes that "all have sinned" (Rom 3:23). In an Orthodox sense, this means our "image of God" has been clouded, and our "likeness" to Him has been lost.
The Ineffective Cure:
He explains that the Mosaic Law (the Old Covenant) acted like a thermometer: it could tell you that you had a fever (sin), but it didn't have the power to break the fever
.
"Therefore by the deeds of the law no flesh will be justified in His sight, for by the law is the knowledge of sin."
(Romans 3:20).
"For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God."
(Romans 3:23)
--- Slide 12 ---
The Medicine: The Blood & Grace (Chapters 4–5)
Once the diagnosis is accepted, the Great Physician provides the remedy.
The Active Ingredient:
Grace (
Tsega
)
. This isn't just a concept; it is the life-giving energy of God.
The Delivery Method:
Faith
. Like a patient trusting a surgeon, we trust in Christ’s sacrifice.
The Outcome:
Paul contrasts Adam and Christ. Adam "infected" the human race with mortality and corruption; Christ "injects" the human race with life and righteousness. We are "justified," which in our theology means being
made right
(healed), not just called right.
"For if when we were enemies we were reconciled to God through the death of His Son, much more, having been reconciled, we shall be saved by His life." (Romans 5:10)
“
But the free gift is not like the offense. For if by the one man’s offense many died, much more the grace of God and the gift by the grace of the one Man, Jesus Christ, abounded to many."
(Romans 5:15)
The Point:
If you are a prisoner in a hospital, you need two things: 1) To be pardoned for your crimes (Reconciliation) and 2) To be cured of your disease (Saved by His Life).
Question:
Why is a "pardon" useless if you are still dying of a terminal illness?
Takeaway:
This is why we need the
Life
of Christ, not just the memory of His death.
--- Slide 13 ---
The Recovery: Baptism & The Spirit (Chapters 6–8)
After a major surgery, a patient needs rehabilitation to regain their strength. This is the stage of
Sanctification
.
The Initial Treatment (Baptism):
In Chapter 6, Paul says we are buried with Christ. In the EOTC, we know that when we emerge from the baptismal waters, the "old, sick man" has died, and a "new, healthy man" is born.
Physical Therapy (The Spirit):
Chapters 7 and 8 describe the struggle between the "flesh" (the lingering habit of sickness) and the "Spirit" (the new health).
The Strength:
Walking in the Spirit—through prayer, fasting, and the Sacraments—is how we stay in "remission" from sin and grow toward
Theosis
(becoming like God).
“
Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead... even so we also should walk in newness of life."
(Romans 6:4)
"For if you live according to the flesh you will die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live."
(Romans 8:13).
--- Slide 14 ---
Community Health: The Living Church (Chapters 12–16)
Finally, a healthy person doesn't live in isolation. They return to the community to serve.
The Body of Christ:
Paul uses the image of a body (Rom 12). If one limb is healthy, it helps the limb that is weak.
Social Hygiene:
This section covers how we treat enemies, how we submit to authorities, and how we handle disagreements (like fasting or holidays).
The Goal:
The "Community Health" is
Love
. St. Paul concludes that a truly healed person is one who can love others as Christ loved us.
"I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your reasonable service."
(Romans 12:1)
"Be kindly affectionate to one another with brotherly love, in honor giving preference to one another."
(Romans 12:10).

--- Slide 16 ---
Oriental Orthodox Perspectives: The Book Of Romans
1. Faith and Works: Two Sides of One Coin
Defining "Works":
Paul’s critique of "works" refers to the
Works of the Law
(Mosaic rituals like circumcision) rather than moral acts of love.
The Synergy:
Faith is not a static belief; it is "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6). Just as the soul and body are one,
Faith and Works are inseparable.
2. Grace as Transformation (Theosis)
Beyond "Legal" Status:
Grace is not merely a legal "pardon" or unmerited favor.
Divine Energy:
It is the
uncreated energy of God
that dwells within us.
The Goal:
We are not just
declared
righteous; we are being actively healed and transformed into the likeness of God (
Theosis
).
3. Unity at the Table
Bearing One Another:
Following Romans 14–15, the "strong" must support the "weak" in love.
The Communion:
Our unity is found at the communal table. We do not let differences in fasting or customs break our
oneness (Tewahedo)
in Christ.
The Command:
"Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you" (Romans 15:7).
Biblical Proof:
Romans 2:6:
"[God] will render to each one according to his deeds."
. This proves faith and works are inseparable
--- Slide 17 ---
Dogma vs Cannon
Dogma: The Unchanging Truth (The Heart)
Dogma
refers to the core, essential beliefs of the Church that are considered divinely revealed and non-negotiable. If you change a dogma, you are no longer part of that faith.
Canon: The Rule of Life (The Fence)
Canon
(from the Greek word for "measuring stick") refers to the laws, rules, and traditions that govern how the Church is organized and how the faithful live out their lives.
--- Slide 18 ---
The Mystery of our Rebirth: From Adam to Christ
The Two Adams (Chapter 5)
This is central to our Christology. The first Adam brought death; the "Second Adam" (Christ) brings life. We inherit the
consequences
of Adam's sin (mortality and corruption), and in Romans 5, Paul explains how Christ reverses this.
Baptism as Participation (Chapter 6)
In the EOTC, we emphasize that Baptism is a real death and rebirth.
"Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life."
(Rom 6:4)
The Spirit-Led Life (Chapter 8)
This is often called the "Inner Life" of the believer. It emphasizes that there is no condemnation for those in Christ. It also highlights that
all creation
groans for redemption—a very Orthodox view of the sanctity of the physical world.
--- Slide 19 ---
The Early Church Fathers: St. John Chrysostom
St. John Chrysostom
had such a deep love for Romans that he had the book read to him twice a week. He famously wrote an entire series of homilies (lessons) on it.
The Quote:
"As I keep hearing the Epistles of the blessed Paul read... I rejoice in the spiritual trumpet and am roused and warmed with desire... For it is from this Epistle [Romans] that we shall best come to know him [Paul] in his entirety."
—
Homilies on Romans, Preface.
The Impact:
He described the experience as hearing a
"spiritual trumpet"
that roused his soul.
Legacy:
Because of this "continual cleaving" to the text, he produced 32 homilies on Romans, which remain the most famous commentary on the book in Church history.
--- Slide 20 ---
The Early Church Fathers: St.Cyril
The Architect of Tewahedo Christology
In the Ethiopian Orthodox tradition, St. Cyril is the primary authority on how the Divine and Human are united in Christ. His commentary on Romans focuses on our
organic union
with the Savior.
The Holy Root and the Branches
"For as we were sentenced to death in Adam, so now we have been justified in Christ... For He [Christ] became our root and first-fruit, and as the root is holy, so also are the branches."
—
Explanation of the Letter to the Romans
The Lesson: Organic Oneness
St. Cyril teaches that salvation is more than just a "legal pardon." It is a biological and spiritual transformation:
Beyond the Courtroom:
We are not just "declared" forgiven while standing far away from God.
The Grafted Life:
We are organically joined to Christ. Just as a branch survives only because it is part of the tree, we have life only because we are "grafted" into the Body of Christ.
The Power of the Root:
If the Root (Christ) is immortal and holy, then the branches (the Faithful) receive that same holiness and immortality through their union (
Tewahedo
) with Him.
